    Where the pitch sits

    House mosquito 380–560 Hz Tiger mosquito ♀ 590–615 Hz Your passes

    Reference bands come from real phone recordings in Stanford's Abuzz dataset, measured with this detector: common house mosquitoes (Culex pipiens) at 380–560 Hz and tiger mosquito females (Aedes albopictus) at 590–615 Hz. Wingbeat pitch rises with temperature and varies between individuals, so treat a match as a hint, not an ID.

    Recording tips

    • Hold the microphone 10–20 cm from where you hear the buzz. Past about 30 cm it sinks into room noise.
    • Turn off fans, AC and music, and stay quiet while recording.
    • Record 20 to 60 seconds, including a few seconds without the buzz.
    • If your recorder has noise reduction or an “enhance” option, switch it off.

    How it decides

    Each 32 ms slice is compared with the recording's own quiet background, so fans and electrical hum drop out. In what's left, it looks for a tone between 280 and 950 Hz whose harmonics stand well clear of the gaps between them, and only counts it if the pitch holds for about a third of a second. Long range averages over almost a second, which picks up a buzz about half as loud (roughly twice the distance) but reacts a bit slower.

    What fools it

    Music, singing and high-pitched voices can look the same. A buzz that never stops for the whole recording can blend into the background. Tested on 14 Abuzz phone recordings of mosquitoes (all detected) and one background-only recording (nothing found).

    Runs entirely in your browser. Recordings longer than 5 minutes are trimmed to the first 5.
